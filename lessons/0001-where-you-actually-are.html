Lesson 01 · Diagnostic

Where you actually are

Two separate abilities, measured separately — because you are almost certainly not at the same level in both.

You asked me not to let you self-report. Good instinct: self-assessment in a second language is notoriously unreliable, and it fails in a specific direction — people who can read a language overestimate how well they can produce it, because recognition feels like knowledge.

So this lesson is a test. But it is also the most efficient kind of lesson there is: every question gives you an answer and a reason immediately. Retrieval followed by feedback beats reading an explanation, even when you get the item wrong — especially when you get it wrong.

What is actually being measured

There are two tracks here, and they are close to independent:

TrackSectionsWhat a low score means
Japanese as a code Kana, Vocabulary & kanji, Grammar Ordinary beginner ground. Fixable by volume. Nobody will be offended by it.
Japanese as social behaviour Register & workplace norms The dangerous one. These errors are silent — colleagues will not correct you, and you will not notice. This is the track your mission actually lives on.

Expect an asymmetry. Most self-taught learners — and every textbook-taught learner I would expect — score far higher on the first track than the second. If that happens to you, it is not a failure. It is the diagnosis, and it is the reason this course is ordered the way it is.

Nothing here is timed. Answer from instinct rather than working it out slowly; instinct is what you will actually have available in a meeting. There are 21 questions and it should take under ten minutes.

The one source to read this week

T1 敬語の指針 — 文化審議会答申, 2 February 2007, 文化庁. PDF (Japanese)

Do not try to read it yet. Open it, look at the table of contents, and notice one thing: the Japanese government found politeness confusing enough that it convened a council and published a formal ruling on it — and that ruling overturned the three-category model that every textbook still teaches. There are five categories, not three.

That document is the backbone of the register track in this course. When a textbook and this disagree, this wins.

What happens next

Paste your result line into the chat. Based on it I will place you and write Lesson 02.

Lesson 02 is already chosen, and it is not kana and not vocabulary. It is the single structural fact that makes 敬語 stop being a memorisation problem: Japanese politeness runs on two independent axes, and English collapses them into one. Almost every register mistake an English speaker makes is a confusion between those two axes. Once you can see them separately, the five categories in the document above stop being a list to memorise and become a grid you can derive.

I am your teacher here, not a document. If any answer above felt arbitrary, or you disagree with one, say so in the chat — disagreement is the most useful thing you can bring me. Several of these have genuinely contested answers, and knowing which ones you push back on tells me more than the score does.
