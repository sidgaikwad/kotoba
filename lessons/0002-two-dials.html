Lesson 02 · Register · The structural insight

Two dials, not one

English has a single politeness knob. Japanese has two, and they move independently. Almost every register mistake you will make is a confusion between them.

First, predict

Do not scroll past this. Answer it in your head, commit to it, and notice how confident you feel.

You are chatting casually with 鈴木さん — your peer, someone you get lunch with. You want to ask whether 田中部長, the department head, has already gone home.

You say:

田中部長、もう帰られた？ Tanaka-buchou, mou kaerareta?

Is this sentence polite or casual?

If you answered "polite," or "casual," or "somewhere in between" — you used one scale, and the question was a trap. The correct answer is both, on different dials, at the same time.

  • 帰られた — honorific. It elevates 田中部長.
  • 〜た？ — plain, casual ending. It is relaxed toward 鈴木さん.

You are being deferential about the person you are discussing while being informal with the person you are talking to. English physically cannot do this in one sentence. That is the whole lesson.

The two dials

Read the top-right cell again. いらっしゃる — honorific about someone, plain toward your listener. That cell is the one English speakers assume does not exist, and it is the cell you will live in constantly: talking to a friend about your boss.

Three things this immediately explains

1. Why a spec is written in plain form and is still formal

An internal 仕様書 uses だ・である. Beginners read that as "informal" and are baffled, because a spec is clearly a formal document.

Resolve it with Dial 1: 丁寧語 is deference toward a listener. A document has no listener in the room. There is nobody to be polite to, so Dial 1 sits at zero — while the document remains entirely formal on every other measure.

Plain is not informal. Polite is not formal. Delete that equation now; it will mislead you for years otherwise.

2. Why you humble your own boss to a client

Dial 2 does not ask "who is senior?" It asks "whose side is each person on, right now?"

Inside ミナト, 田中部長 outranks you, so Dial 2 elevates him. The instant you speak to 株式会社ケイヨウ, your whole company collapses into 内 — your side — and the client becomes 外. 田中 is now on your side of the line, so he takes the same treatment you give yourself: no title, no さん, humble verbs.

田中はただいま外出しております。 Tanaka wa tadaima gaishutsu shite orimasu. To a client. Dial 1 high — polite to them. Dial 2 humble — 田中 is now mine.
田中部長はいらっしゃいません。 Tanaka-buchou wa irasshaimasen. To a client: wrong. Elevating your own side above the customer.

The sentences are not "more" and "less" polite. Dial 1 is identical in both. Only Dial 2 flipped, because the boundary of "my side" moved.

3. Why 二重敬語 is an error rather than extra safety

おっしゃられる is おっしゃる (already Dial 2) plus -られる (Dial 2 again). It turns one dial twice. That is not more respect — it is a mechanism misfiring, the way a doubled negation is not a stronger no.

You will hear native speakers do this daily. 敬語の指針 still names it as something to avoid. Both facts are true and you should hold both.

The payoff: why there are five categories, not three

Once Dial 2 exists as its own thing, the split that confuses everyone becomes derivable. 謙譲語 lowers you — but toward whom? There are two different answers, so there are two different categories.

謙譲語Ⅰ謙譲語Ⅱ 丁重語
Lowers me toward A third party in the sentence The listener
Needs A target worthy of respect Nothing — just a listener
Example 部長のところに伺います
I visit the department head		 明日、大阪に参ります
I go to Osaka tomorrow
Why Elevates 部長 by lowering my approach to him Osaka cannot be respected. The humility is aimed at you, the listener

The old three-category model had no slot for the second one, which is why the 文化審議会 revised it in 2007. You did not memorise that distinction — you derived it from the dials.
